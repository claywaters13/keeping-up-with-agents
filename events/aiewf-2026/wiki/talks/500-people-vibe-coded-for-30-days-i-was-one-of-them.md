# 500 people vibe-coded for 30 days. I was one of them.

**Speakers:** [Sanja Grbic](../speakers/sanja-grbic.md)

**Org:** Automattic

**Duration:** 17m 48s

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8)

## Summary

Sanja Grbic, a product designer at Automattic, recounts "Radical Speed Month" — a company experiment where roadmap work was paused for 30 days and two-person teams were given full autonomy to build and ship something real. About a third of the company (~500 people) participated in the first round, starting roughly 794 projects. She walks through three projects she personally built, tracing her shift from a designer who had never shipped code at work to someone deploying an internal tool to production in a large, security-gated system. The talk's real argument is organizational rather than technical: AI velocity in a 1,400-person company with decades-old software depends less on the tools than on enablement, agency, and space to experiment. Watch it if you care about how non-engineers actually cross into code inside a big org, and what engineers' roles become when they do.

## Key Points

- Automattic paused roadmap work for 30 days and had ~500 people (a third of the company, in staged rounds) pair up and ship real projects with full autonomy; AI use was encouraged but not required.
- The enabling infrastructure predated the experiment: a two-week role-specific AI course for every employee, security and dev-environment setup good enough for non-engineers, and an MCP server ("context AC") exposing decades of internal documentation to AI tools.
- In a two-hour group exercise, a mixed team of two designers, an engineer, and a product lead shipped a working board-game session manager using Claude Code plus Nano Banana for illustration — with the engineer's main contribution being to set up Git and unblock the others.
- Grbic's central claim for engineers: enabling teammates who are newly working in code can produce more impact than doing more engineering yourself.
- Her main project, a design system status tracker, was proposed over an engineer's concerns about performance, maintenance, and feasibility; she built it solo as a proof of concept in about 2.5 weeks and shipped it internally and on a public-facing site.
- The process inversion is the concrete takeaway: prototype in code first, then return to Figma late for mood boards and visual fine-tuning, rather than working in Figma to high fidelity before building.
- The third project — an iOS chat app for WooCommerce merchants with wordpress.com auth, a site-themed widget, and an AI agent that answers visitor questions and knows when it can't — went zero to working proof of concept in six days, because both designers had already leveled up.
- Her prescription for large organizations: give people tool access, find enablers and champions, create space for experimentation, and grant agency — because changing process in a big company means changing human behavior, not just tooling.

## Positions

- An engineer's impact from enabling non-engineer teammates can exceed the impact of doing more engineering themselves. ([8:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=481s), confidence: stated)
- Engineers' roles will need to shift from builders to enablers and teachers as more non-engineers work in code. ([9:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=542s), confidence: stated)
- The primary enabler of her shift to design engineer was owning the entire process and having agency, more than the AI tooling itself. ([12:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=732s), confidence: stated)
- AI-driven velocity looks fundamentally different in 1-3 person zero-to-one teams than in organizations of thousands with 5-20 year old software. ([2:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=156s), confidence: stated)
- Designers should build the working prototype first and return to Figma only later for visual fine-tuning, inverting the traditional high-fidelity-mockup-first workflow. ([14:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=893s), confidence: stated)
- Recording exploration chats and ideas into a Claude Code project folder file system significantly speeds up both collaboration and subsequent building. ([14:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=893s), confidence: stated)
- Adopting AI tools in a large organization is a human behavior change problem, so tool access alone is insufficient without champions, experimentation space, and agency. ([17:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=1032s), confidence: stated)
- Removing risk from a project (building something unrelated to existing products) is what allowed the team to focus on learning. ([8:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=481s), confidence: stated)
- Well-documented asynchronous work practices are a prerequisite for making internal knowledge usefully accessible to AI tools. ([0:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=50s), confidence: implied)
- A non-engineer can ship to production in a large legacy system when systems and operations teams have invested in security, process, and documentation quality. ([3:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UcYoMg-8-L8&t=198s), confidence: stated)
