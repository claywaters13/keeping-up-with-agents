# deep tech company building

**Maturity: CONSOLIDATING** — Consolidating — converging practice, some open edges

*Core concept* &middot; discussed across **4** talk(s) by **4** speaker(s)

**Definition:** Building startups whose core risk is scientific or physical rather than market — long R&D timelines, capital intensity, manufacturing, and the strategy choices those force.

*Also referred to as: hard tech startups, vertical integration, product-market fit in capital-intensive hardware, brain-computer interfaces, quantum information science*

## State of Practice

The 2026 view is that deep tech failure is organizational and operational, not scientific — the technology usually works, and what kills companies is iteration cost and an inability to run a human organization at speed. Practitioners now treat the company's internal machinery (purchasing, recruiting, budgeting, performance review, in-house machine shops and test stands) as the actual product-development bottleneck, and they measure themselves in learning cycles per unit time rather than in milestones. The strongest current move is to make atoms behave like bits: hardware engineering that lives in spreadsheets gets rewritten as versioned code with CI and automated testing, and suppliers whose lead times set your cycle get insourced even at high capital cost. Regulation has flipped from an assumed wall to a movable constraint — build the thing, show why it's safe, and either bring the regulator onto the team or ship first depending on whether an incumbent is weaponizing them. Credentialism is out: expertise is discounted as backward-looking, and hiring filters have moved to judgment, agency, caring, and AI-tool fluency. On capital, the emerging discipline is to price the experiments to the next value inflection, raise roughly twice that, and find intermediate revenue early so the company is valued on its roadmap rather than its probability of dying.

## Disagreements

### Is the regulation a deep tech company faces mostly legitimate accumulated safety knowledge, or mostly incumbent protectionism?

| Position A | Position B |
|---|---|
| Most regulations are written in blood and are largely good ideas; the real bottleneck is that humans are bad at reading and interpreting standards, which makes compliance an AI-tractable problem rather than a political one.<br>*[Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md)* | Most regulation originates from incumbents using government to block competition rather than from public-safety concern — 'deeply unethical' — and whole technologies (supersonic flight) are stagnant because of rules that regulate the wrong variable.<br>*[How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet](../talks/how-50-people-built-a-supersonic-jet.md), [Inside the White House's AI Strategy](../talks/inside-the-white-houses-ai-strategy.md)* |

*Why it matters: If the rules are legitimate, you staff a quality/regulatory function and automate interpretation; if they are captured, you spend founder time on political and legislative change and consider launching before the regulator is ready. The two strategies consume completely different resources and produce different company cultures.*

### Does a hard-tech idea succeed because enabling conditions have finally arrived, or simply because someone decided to do it?

| Position A | Position B |
|---|---|
| 'Why now' misleads founders — many multi-billion-dollar ideas were technically feasible years before anyone started them, and supersonic flight was one; the binding constraint was that nobody went and did it.<br>*[How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet](../talks/how-50-people-built-a-supersonic-jet.md)* | Great startups cluster when the ecosystem shifts, costs fall, cycle times shorten and incumbents lose their advantage; identifying the wave early (AI in 2017-18, quantum now) is the substance of the bet.<br>*[Never a Better Time to Do a Startup](../talks/never-a-better-time-to-do-a-startup.md), [Inside the White House's AI Strategy](../talks/inside-the-white-houses-ai-strategy.md)* |

*Why it matters: It determines whether you screen ideas by timing signals and enabling-technology curves, or by scanning for large obvious problems nobody has attempted regardless of when they became feasible — and whether being ten years early is a fatal error or the whole opportunity.*

## Practical Guidance

**Do:**

- Rewrite hardware engineering that currently lives in spreadsheets as real code with automated testing and continuous integration.
- Build your own machine shop and test stands rather than depending on outside suppliers whose lead times set your iteration cycle, even at the capital cost.
- When two technical approaches are otherwise comparable, take the one with the shorter iteration loop — compounding dominates every other advantage.
- Price out the experiments needed to reach the next value inflection point and raise twice that amount; treat 20-30% waste as a good outcome.
- Find intermediate revenue on the way to the long-horizon product (Boom sold its supersonic engine as a ground-based natural gas power turbine) so you are valued on your roadmap rather than your probability of dying.
- Build the smallest prototype that gives multiple shots on goal, and iterate at the product/integration level rather than perfecting subsystems.
- Bring regulators onto the team early for safety-critical products with no active opponent; ship first when an entrenched incumbent is weaponizing the regulator.
- Keep interview-to-offer conversion at 25% or better, or on-sites burn too much of the team's time.
- Make take-homes AI-resistant by using non-saturating, high-ceiling, numerically scorable tasks, and stop policing candidates' model use.
- Staff roughly 20-30% mission zealots against 70-80% pragmatic professionals; both halves are load-bearing over a long horizon.
- Do something about spending, hiring and review religiously and consistently as a company — the specific system matters less than having one.

**Avoid:**

- Controlling burn at the point of individual purchase approval — waiting a week to get a power supply half off destroys far more value than it saves. Do the review at the budgeting stage.
- Making your first prototype maximally ambitious: XB-1 took ten years where a simpler design would have reached proof of concept in two to three.
- Treating per-iteration cost as fixed ($40k per wafer) without separating the experiments that are genuinely cheap from the ones that are not.
- Annual 360 performance reviews as the primary signal — they are disruptive and mostly resurface problems you already knew about.
- Hiring on credentials or deep domain expertise; assuming a PhD is better preparation than time at a high-performing adjacent company.
- Building physical-world manufacturing at scale on a Bay Area cost base without a specific reason to be there.
- Vibe-coding core business systems immediately after a Series A without board buy-in — a reasonable board will ask what you are thinking.
- Setting fixed regulatory thresholds early (compute thresholds, the EU AI Act finalized before ChatGPT existed) — once government sets a line it is very hard to reset.
- Starting the same company everyone else is starting: easy hype and easy money, rarely the large outcome.

## Notable Outliers

- Deep tech startups rarely fail because the technology doesn't work — they fail because they cannot organize the human organization to accomplish the goal, and that is a learnable skill. ([Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md), [25:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xc4klGbq8v8&t=1543s))
- Continuous peer voting weighted by eigenvector centrality — asking 'knowing how this person turned out, would you vote again today for their hire?' — is more or less the right way to do performance reviews. ([Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md), [17:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xc4klGbq8v8&t=1058s))
- Deep expertise is not merely overrated in hardware but actively harmful, and it is fine for a young engineer to do something they have never done before exactly as it is fine for them to do something nobody has ever done. ([How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet](../talks/how-50-people-built-a-supersonic-jet.md), [43:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2593s))
- America cannot beat China by repatriating the manufacturing that left; it has to invent the next generation of manufacturing, such as tooling-free digital processes for turbine blades. ([How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet](../talks/how-50-people-built-a-supersonic-jet.md), [22:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=1373s))
- Biotech is capital intensive enough that he would not recommend it to someone with other options, despite the patient impact being unlike any other sector. ([Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md), [53:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xc4klGbq8v8&t=3217s))
- The Vannevar Bush model of federal science funding is obsolete because the shares inverted — government fell from ~70% of US R&D in 1950 to ~30%, with private sector plus philanthropy now at ~70% and near parity even in basic research. ([Inside the White House's AI Strategy](../talks/inside-the-white-houses-ai-strategy.md), [27:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1669s))
- A small team that can hold an interdisciplinary problem in their heads beats academia's model of verticalized experts meeting at an interdisciplinary center, because the latter ships the interfaces between departments. ([Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md), [33:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xc4klGbq8v8&t=2005s))
