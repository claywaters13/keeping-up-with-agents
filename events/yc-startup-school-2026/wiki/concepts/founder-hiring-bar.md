# founder hiring bar

**Maturity: CONSOLIDATING** — Consolidating — converging practice, some open edges

*Core concept* &middot; discussed across **3** talk(s) by **3** speaker(s)

**Definition:** How early-stage companies find, assess, and evaluate people — funnel design, work-sample assessment, who holds the hiring decision, and post-hire performance evaluation.

*Also referred to as: hiring funnel design, ai-resistant take-home assessments, hiring early-career talent, distributed hiring judgment, performance review design, co-founder matching*

## State of Practice

Across these talks the hiring bar has moved off credentials and role-specific skill and onto a small set of traits the speakers believe cannot be taught: judgment, agency, raw horsepower, caring, and fluency with AI tools. Hodak operationalizes this most concretely — a four-stage funnel (company-wide vote on judgment/horsepower/agency, phone screen, take-home, on-site), a take-home deliberately built to be non-saturating, high-ceiling, and numerically scorable so that candidate AI use stops mattering, an interview-to-offer conversion floor of 25%, and continuous peer voting weighted by eigenvector centrality in place of annual 360 reviews, with the load-bearing question being 'knowing how this person turned out, would you vote again today for their hire?' Scholl runs the opposite-shaped process — one dominant filter, caring, on the theory that knowledge is changeable and passion is not, and treats deep aerospace expertise as actively disqualifying because experts are steeped in the past. Altman's contribution is directional rather than procedural: AI fluency will out-predict many years of domain experience for who builds the next set of large companies. All three treat recruiting as operating infrastructure rather than an HR function — Hodak's claim is that speed determines success, infrastructure determines speed, and recruiting is part of that infrastructure, and that startups more often fail at organizing humans than at making the technology work. Notably, nobody here defends the credential-and-experience screen; the live argument is about what replaces it and how heavyweight the assessment process should be.

## Consensus

### Years of domain experience and formal credentials are weak or actively negative hiring signals; the bar should be set on traits that cannot be taught — judgment, agency, caring, and fluency with current tools.

## Disagreements

### What evidence should carry the most weight that a candidate is exceptional — a legible track record of competitive wins, or demonstrated caring about the problem?

| Position A | Position B |
|---|---|
| Winning legible competitive games is the best available evidence of exceptional ability, and it is hard to know you are exceptional without one; the funnel should be a structured, multi-stage, numerically scored assessment (company-wide vote, phone screen, scorable take-home, on-site) with an explicit conversion floor.<br>*[Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md)* | Caring is the primary filter, ranked above skills, knowledge, and credentials, because it is the one thing that cannot be taught; expertise and track record in the domain are discounted or treated as harmful, and young engineers should be handed work nobody has done before.<br>*[How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet](../talks/how-50-people-built-a-supersonic-jet.md)* |

*Why it matters: The two produce different funnels: one invests heavily in scorable work-sample assessment and a company-wide bar, the other invests in reading motivation and is willing to hire people with no relevant record at all. It also changes who is even sourceable — legible-wins screening narrows the top of funnel to people with formal competitive history, which the caring filter deliberately does not.*

## Practical Guidance

**Do:**

- Open the funnel with a company-wide vote scored on exactly three things — judgment, horsepower, and agency — rather than role-specific skill
- Hold interview-to-offer conversion at 25% or better; below that, on-sites are consuming more team time than the signal justifies
- Build take-homes that are non-saturating, high-ceiling, and numerically scorable, then stop caring whether candidates used AI models
- Design assessments around whether the candidate can think clearly, not whether they can write code
- Replace annual 360 reviews with continuous peer voting weighted by eigenvector centrality, anchored on the question 'knowing how this person turned out, would you vote again today for their hire?'
- Filter for caring above skills, knowledge, and credentials, on the theory that knowledge is changeable and passion is not
- Give young engineers work they have never done — including work nobody has done — instead of reserving it for domain experts
- Deliberately staff a mix of mission zealots and pragmatic professionals (roughly 20/80 at SpaceX, 30/70 at Science) rather than hiring only true believers
- Hire more software engineers as AI drops the cost of code, because someone has to keep the architecture coherent when everyone can code
- Treat recruiting, purchasing, budgeting, and performance review as the company's operating system, because these determine iteration speed
- Prefer a stint at a high-performing company adjacent to your target area over a PhD, since startup practice is an oral tradition learned by doing

**Avoid:**

- Treating deep domain expertise as the qualification — the moment someone becomes an expert, what they are steeped in is the past
- Running annual 360 performance reviews, which are disruptive and mostly resurface problems everyone already knew about
- Trying to detect or police candidate AI use instead of redesigning the take-home so AI use does not invalidate the signal
- Running on-sites at low offer-conversion rates, which burns the team's time on candidates who were never close
- Assuming the company will fail on technology — it is more often failure to organize the human organization
- As CEO, delegating judgment on hires or anything else because momentum has built behind an alternative
- Buying commercial ATS/ERP products that force their bottlenecks onto your process when the process can now be built internally
- Skipping steps in the funnel in search of efficiency — the four stages are the minimum needed to make a hiring decision
