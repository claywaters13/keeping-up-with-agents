# contrarian conviction

**Maturity: CONSOLIDATING** — Consolidating — converging practice, some open edges

*Core concept* &middot; discussed across **5** talk(s) by **5** speaker(s)

**Definition:** Holding and acting on a belief the market or peers reject, including how founders reason from first principles to earn that belief and when stubbornness becomes delusion.

*Also referred to as: founder judgment, schlep blindness, first-principles thought experiments*

## State of Practice

Every speaker at this conference treated differentiated belief as the entry ticket rather than a personality quirk: the consensus idea is available to everyone, gets funded easily, and rarely produces the large outcome. The interesting work has moved to the calibration problem — what evidence licenses years of conviction before the market agrees, and what distinguishes early from wrong. Three distinct falsification tests were proposed and they are not the same test: a concrete, viscerally felt customer problem you are already serving (Collison), a quantitative capability gate where general models succeed 0-1% rather than 20% of the time (Dean), and social calibration on whether anyone else at all shares the belief (Altman). Notably, AI capability change has shifted the default: Collison argues lean-startup incrementalism was a response to capital scarcity and that the most successful companies of the last decade were explicitly anti-lean, while Wang and Altman both argue the correct posture is to pick the steepest, longest exponential and toil in obscurity until it becomes consensus. What nobody defends is conviction without a metabolism for evidence — Hodak's 'differentiatedly good judgment' comes packaged with knowing the boundaries of your judgment, and Collison's two-year pre-launch came packaged with a paying customer at month two.

## Disagreements

### How long may a founder act on a contrarian belief before real users validate it?

| Position A | Position B |
|---|---|
| Years. The core idea is supposed to be non-consensus, so you should expect to toil in obscurity long before the market agrees, and the right unit of selection is the exponential with the steepest curve and longest runway — even if its starting point looks boring and nobody funds it.<br>*[This is a Once-in-a-Civilization Opportunity](../talks/this-is-a-once-in-a-civilization-opportunity.md), [Never a Better Time to Do a Startup](../talks/never-a-better-time-to-do-a-startup.md)* | A long pre-launch period is defensible only with a continuous stream of real production users throughout; Stripe waited two years to launch but had a paying customer at month two and added customers every month, and outside heavily regulated infrastructure, waiting two years is the wrong call.<br>*[Is AI Breaking the Lean Startup Playbook?](../talks/is-ai-breaking-the-lean-startup-playbook.md)* |

*Why it matters: This sets the burn plan and the milestone structure of the first raise: an unvalidated multi-year build needs a research-style budget and investor base, while the continuous-users model forces distribution work in month one. It also determines which internal signal counts as evidence you were wrong.*

### Is the absence of anyone who agrees with you evidence that you are wrong, or the point?

| Position A | Position B |
|---|---|
| Treat it as a warning. If you cannot find a single other person who shares the belief, pay attention to that — universal agreement is also a bad sign, so the target is a small set of credible believers, not zero.<br>*[Never a Better Time to Do a Startup](../talks/never-a-better-time-to-do-a-startup.md)* | Peer belief is not the test. *Why it matters: It determines what you do when investors and advisors all pass: recruit believers and re-examine the thesis, or discount the signal entirely and keep building. ### Does a contrarian bet need an explicit durability test against improving general models?

| Position A | Position B |
|---|---|
| Yes, and it should be quantitative and gating: pick problems where the general model succeeds 0% or 1% of the time, not 20%, since partial success means the capability is already emerging; then ask whether your narrow-domain advantage lasts years rather than months.<br>*[The 1% Rule for Building in AI](../talks/the-1-rule-for-building-in-ai.md)* | Largely no. *Why it matters: Dean's gate rules out a large fraction of the AI-wrapper bets the others' framing encourages, and it changes what you build first: a defensible data or surface advantage versus speed of diffusion into an underserved market.*

## Practical Guidance

**Do:**

- Pick problems where today's general models succeed 0% or 1% of the time; ~20% success means the capability is emerging and will be absorbed by scale
- Before raising significant money, evaluate the success case, not just the failure case — whether you want to do this specific work for 10, 17, or 30 years
- If pre-launch will be long, keep a continuous stream of real production users throughout (Stripe: two years to launch, paying customer at month two, new customers every month)
- Train taste deliberately: write down what you think will matter in 12 months, then grade later which predictions came true
- When two approaches are otherwise comparable, take the one with the shorter iteration cycle — a weekly learner beats a monthly learner outright
- Identify the exponential with both the steepest curve and the longest runway, even if its starting point looks boring
- Price out the experiments needed to reach the next value inflection point and raise roughly twice that; 20-30% waste is a good outcome
- Check whether a narrow-domain advantage (surface design, targeted skills, a specialized model, private data) is durable for years rather than months
- Push toward revenue earlier than feels necessary even in long-horizon deep tech, so you are valued on your roadmap rather than your probability of dying
- Staff the mission with both zealots and pragmatists — roughly 30/70 at Science, 20/80 at SpaceX

**Avoid:**

- Starting the same company as everyone else because it draws hype and easy money; those are rarely the big outcomes
- Making business decisions by reading the herd — going too much with the crowd leaves you confused and nowhere
- Building on an imagined customer problem rather than one you have viscerally felt
- Spending time arguing about superintelligence timelines or whether progress hits a wall; powerful models are coming either way
- Delegating judgment as CEO — years in you will hit a decision with nobody left to ask, and you need to know the boundaries of your own judgment by then
- Treating dropping out or an early failure as a permanent reputational cost; as far as anyone can tell, nobody has ever cared
- Taking public shots at people building things — it accomplishes nothing over a decade and corrodes the attacker
- Going all in on verbal ability at the expense of quantitative and systems thinking, which survives every shift in abstraction layer
- Assuming a contrarian technical bet fails on technology — deep tech companies more often fail at organizing humans

## Notable Outliers

- Public derision was net-positive for OpenAI: being called idiots for years deterred serious competitors and bought research time — the mockery was a moat, not a cost. ([Never a Better Time to Do a Startup](../talks/never-a-better-time-to-do-a-startup.md), [12:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZIaOBAjvc38&t=778s))
- The most durable dystopia risk is overreacting to AI safety and landing in abundance plus total surveillance — a contrarian position inside the safety conversation itself. ([Never a Better Time to Do a Startup](../talks/never-a-better-time-to-do-a-startup.md), [36:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZIaOBAjvc38&t=2199s))
- The distillation paper was rejected as unlikely to have significant impact, and it is now part of why Gemini Flash models are so capable for their size — peer rejection tracked nothing about eventual impact. ([The 1% Rule for Building in AI](../talks/the-1-rule-for-building-in-ai.md), [48:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxXgV54KzpQ&t=2930s))
- The best evidence you are exceptional is having won a legible competitive game; without one it is hard to know, and a high-performing adjacent company beats a PhD as preparation because startup culture is an oral tradition. ([Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md), [27:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xc4klGbq8v8&t=1622s))
- Companies should build internal ERP/ATS software rather than buy it now that agents make it viable — while conceding that telling your board post-Series A that you plan to vibe code a purchasing system will reasonably get you questioned. ([Average Is Not Good Enough](../talks/average-is-not-good-enough.md), [38:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xc4klGbq8v8&t=2290s))
- Chip design's 60-year assumption of near-perfect transistors is worth abandoning; reliability could be handled at a higher level, as distributed storage systems already do. ([The 1% Rule for Building in AI](../talks/the-1-rule-for-building-in-ai.md), [37:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxXgV54KzpQ&t=2258s))
