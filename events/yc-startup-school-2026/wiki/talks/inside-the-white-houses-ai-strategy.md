# Inside the White House's AI Strategy

**Speakers:** [Michael Kratsios](../speakers/michael-kratsios.md)

**Org:** White House Office of Science and Technology Policy

**Duration:** 39m 44s

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU)

## Summary

Michael Kratsios, director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy and one of the authors of the national AI Action Plan, is interviewed at YC Startup School about how AI policy actually gets made. His core argument is that technology policy in the US is deliberately federated across agencies, that hard regulatory thresholds (the EU AI Act, the Biden compute cap) age badly against a fast-moving frontier, and that the administration's priorities are open-source support, federal preemption of a state patchwork, and removing barriers for 'born in captivity' technologies that need government approval to commercialize. He also walks through a new ~150-page report to the president on revitalizing US science, arguing AI will reshape scientific discovery and that autonomous cloud labs are the infrastructure gap startups should fill. Worth watching if you want a concrete read on where federal AI regulation is headed and why the White House says small companies, not incumbents, are the constituency it is optimizing for — plus a rare walkthrough of how White House policy processes escalate to the president.

## Key Points

- The White House position on open source is unchanged from chapter one of the July 2026 AI Action Plan: US leadership requires both a vibrant closed and open-source ecosystem, and the week's rumored crackdown EO did not reflect policy.
- Kratsios argues against firm regulatory thresholds because they do not survive the frontier — citing the EU AI Act, finalized before ChatGPT existed, and the prior administration's hard compute-threshold disclosure trigger.
- Federal preemption of state AI law is a top priority: a patchwork across California, Maryland, and Texas is survivable for Google's lawyers but fatal for two-person startups, so preemption is framed as a little-tech issue rather than a deregulation issue.
- He splits technologies into 'born free' (no regulations on the books, e.g. the early internet — preserve that state) and 'born in captivity' (cannot be commercialized without government approval, e.g. commercial drones, supersonic flight — relentlessly remove barriers).
- On near-term AI risk he ranks cyber capability as the live concern, noting the same model that can act nefariously is the one that hardens systems, while calling biological risk overblown — people were warning about it in 2021-22 and it has not been an issue for three years.
- The funding structure of American science has inverted since 1950: the federal government went from roughly 70% of R&D to roughly 30%, with private sector plus philanthropy now at 70% and near parity even in basic research, which is the premise of the new report.
- The report's forward-looking bet is autonomous cloud labs — AI agents proposing hypotheses, running experiments robotically, and iterating without human intervention — with robotics and the hypothesis-generation software layer named as the open startup opportunities.
- On IP, he draws a clear line at model outputs (a model emitting Mickey Mouse is not acceptable) while arguing licensing and revenue-sharing markets for creators are too immature to legislate and should be left to develop.
- Quantum information science is his pick for the technology Washington will care enormously about in five years, with a presidential EO signed and a Department of Energy goal of a scientifically relevant quantum computer by 2028.

## Positions

- US AI leadership requires both a closed and an open-source ecosystem, and support for open source is the first item in chapter one of the administration's AI Action Plan. ([7:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=429s), confidence: stated)
- Hard regulatory red lines for AI do not survive contact with the frontier; the EU AI Act was finalized before ChatGPT existed and now governs LLMs it never anticipated. ([9:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=595s), confidence: stated)
- The prior administration's fixed compute threshold for mandatory government disclosure was a mistake of the same kind, and this administration is deliberately cautious about setting such thresholds. ([11:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=670s), confidence: stated)
- Once government sets a regulatory line it is very hard to reset, so the cost of setting one too early is higher than the cost of waiting. ([11:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=670s), confidence: stated)
- Biological risk from AI models has been overblown for years — warnings began in 2021-22 and it has not been an issue for three years — while cyber capability is the more real present risk. ([12:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=767s), confidence: stated)
- Test and eval infrastructure for models crossing the frontier still needs to be built, even if current risk claims are overstated. ([12:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=767s), confidence: stated)
- A state-by-state patchwork of AI regulation functions as a subsidy to large incumbents, who can absorb the legal cost, and as a barrier to startups who cannot. ([15:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=930s), confidence: stated)
- A single national AI standard passed by Congress would be the single biggest regulatory boon available to startups. ([17:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1077s), confidence: stated)
- No administration in history has been more committed to reducing regulations than the current one. ([17:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1077s), confidence: stated)
- Regulatory strategy should differ by category: preserve the unregulated state of 'born free' technologies and aggressively remove approval barriers for 'born in captivity' ones. ([18:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1122s), confidence: stated)
- Supersonic flight is the clearest example of technological stagnation caused by rules — the fix is regulating noise rather than speed over the United States. ([20:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1216s), confidence: stated)
- The Vannevar Bush model of federal science funding is outdated because the funding shares inverted: government fell from ~70% of R&D in 1950 to ~30%, with private sector plus philanthropy now at ~70% and near parity in basic research. ([27:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1669s), confidence: stated)
- AI will fundamentally change how scientific discovery is done within two to three years across materials science, pharmaceuticals, and chemistry, and the ~$200B/year federal R&D budget should be prioritized accordingly. ([28:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1739s), confidence: stated)
- The government's role is to shape the arena rather than direct discovery, leaving robotics for cloud labs and hypothesis-generation software as startup territory. ([30:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1818s), confidence: stated)
- Quantum information science is where AI was in 2017-18 in Washington — underdiscussed relative to its importance — and the administration is targeting a scientifically relevant quantum computer at DOE by 2028. ([32:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=1942s), confidence: stated)
- Executive orders are a weaker instrument than statute because a successor administration can simply revoke them, which is why preemption should be legislated. ([34:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=2065s), confidence: stated)
- Model outputs that reproduce protected IP such as Mickey Mouse should be prohibited, and that prohibition should be clarified in statute. ([36:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=2174s), confidence: stated)
- Creator licensing and revenue-sharing markets for AI training should be left to mature on their own rather than legislated now. ([37:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=2248s), confidence: stated)
- Founder and startup-community pressure on Washington — like the week's open-weights letters — improves policy outcomes rather than merely creating noise. ([7:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zLUZclThLhU&t=460s), confidence: stated)
