# How 50 People Built a Supersonic Jet

**Speakers:** [Blake Scholl](../speakers/blake-scholl.md)

**Org:** Boom Supersonic

**Duration:** 50m 01s

[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs)

## Summary

Blake Scholl tells the story of Boom Supersonic's XB-1 — the first independently developed supersonic jet — built by a team of 50 rather than the thousands and billions such programs historically required. His central engineering argument is that hardware teams should make development look like software development: move discipline-specific 'spreadsheet engineering' into real code with simulation and CI (their Makeboom and Bladerunner tools), and vertically integrate machine shops and test stands so the physical iteration loop drops from months to about 24 hours. The second half is a long founder Q&A covering why 'why now?' is a trap, how Boom got supersonic flight re-legalized by making the FAA a partner rather than an adversary, why America beats China by inventing the next generation of manufacturing instead of repatriating the last one, and why the cost of software falling means Boom needs more software engineers, not fewer. Worth watching if you want a deep-tech counterweight to software-startup orthodoxy — especially on regulated industries, hiring for care over credentials, and financing a decade-long R&D program (Boom sells its supersonic engine as a ground-based gas turbine to fund the airplane).

## Key Points

- XB-1 broke the sound barrier six times across two flights with no audible boom on the ground, demonstrating 'Mach cutoff' — breaking the sound barrier high enough and at the right speed for the weather so the boom refracts upward and never reaches the surface.
- That demonstration converted a regulatory dead end into policy: 24 hours from the flight to a West Wing invitation, 115 days to an executive order re-legalizing US supersonic flight, followed by unanimous House passage and a unanimous Senate Commerce Committee vote.
- Boom moved integrated aircraft engineering out of siloed Excel spreadsheets into code — a system called Makeboom defines an airplane in a config file and simulates whole-aircraft performance in minutes, letting them search the design space for product-market fit before building anything.
- Vertical integration is the atoms-side equivalent: their own R&D machine shop gets them from digital design to prototype part in ~24 hours, and their own engine test stand removes the wait for someone else's test cell — Scholl says outside aerospace suppliers make iteration nearly impossible.
- Boom financed the airplane by selling its supersonic engine separately as a natural-gas ground power turbine ('Superpower'), generating test data, reliability learnings, and capital; first customer delivery in under 12 months.
- Scholl rejects the 'why now?' framing that concentrates founders on recently-unlocked ideas: supersonic was technically feasible a decade before Boom existed, and the honest answer to why now can simply be 'because I started now.'
- On regulation, the right strategy is contextual — Uber was right to build first because an entrenched taxi industry was weaponizing regulators, but for safety-critical aviation with no active opponent, engaging the FAA early turned a months-long approval into 90 minutes.
- On hiring, Boom favors early-career people 'not yet destroyed' by legacy aerospace and promotes from within at senior levels; the one thing you cannot teach is caring, and the claim that hardware requires decades of experience is 'a lie told by old people at big dumb companies.'
- Boom's rule for inexperienced engineers: doing something you've never done is fine, and doing something nobody has ever done is fine, but if someone in the world has done it, you must call them and hear their advice — you just don't have to take it.

## Positions

- Hardware engineering that currently lives in spreadsheets should be rewritten as real software with automated testing and continuous integration, because that is what collapses iteration cost. ([13:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=829s), confidence: stated)
- You can change regulations by building the thing and explaining why it's safe — the objection to building in regulated industries is overstated. ([10:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=652s), confidence: stated)
- Whether to engage regulators early or launch first is contextual: launch-first works when an entrenched incumbent is weaponizing the regulator, engage-first works for safety-critical products with no active opponent. ([28:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=1686s), confidence: stated)
- Most regulation originates not from public-safety concern but from incumbents using government to block competition, which he calls deeply unethical. ([28:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=1723s), confidence: stated)
- The 'why now' question misleads founders; many multi-billion-dollar ideas are technically feasible years before anyone starts them, and supersonic flight was one of them. ([20:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=1245s), confidence: stated)
- America cannot beat China by repatriating the manufacturing that left; it must invent the next generation of manufacturing, e.g. tooling-free digital processes for turbine blades. ([22:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=1373s), confidence: stated)
- Outside aerospace suppliers make fast iteration impossible, so building your own machine shop and test stands is worth the capital cost. ([15:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=946s), confidence: stated)
- Boom's need for software engineers has increased as AI lowered the cost of software, because someone must keep architectures coherent when everyone can code. ([41:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2510s), confidence: stated)
- The premise that experience wins in hardware is false, and deep expertise is actively harmful because expertise is steeped in the past. ([43:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2593s), confidence: stated)
- Boom's prototype (XB-1) was too ambitious; a simpler prototype giving multiple shots on goal would have reached proof of concept in two to three years instead of ten. ([33:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2016s), confidence: stated)
- Caring cannot be taught, so it should be the primary hiring filter — above skills, knowledge, and credentials. ([30:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=1833s), confidence: stated)
- Founders do not need to be in San Francisco — Boom left for Denver because physical-world manufacturing at scale is not affordable in the Bay Area — but should be in some ambitious ecosystem absent a specific reason. ([46:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2763s), confidence: stated)
- Work-life balance is the wrong frame; work-life harmony is achievable, and being a parent and a CEO are synergistic rather than opposed. ([48:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2922s), confidence: stated)
- Amazon's advantage came from pairing seven-year planning horizons with day-to-day operational excellence and refusing to massage quarterly results. ([38:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2301s), confidence: stated)
- Self-belief is not a prerequisite for starting something audacious; it arrives late, and the right move is to redirect mental energy from 'can I' to 'how.' ([37:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=byAj35QlGbs&t=2232s), confidence: stated)
